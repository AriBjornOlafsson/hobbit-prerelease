I. Overview

This is a small set — only five supported color-pair archetypes exist, rather than the usual wider spread. Know your five pairs before you sit down to build or draft, because straying outside them tends to leave a deck without a real plan.

Expect this to play closer to a set like Marvel Superheroes than to weaker small sets like Teenage Mutant Ninja Turtles or Spider-Man — but with fewer late-game mana sinks. There isn't much to spend excess mana on once you're deep into a game, so card advantage and efficient curves matter more than big splashy top-end plays. This set plays a notch above normal power level at every rarity, commons included — if a Hobbit common feels unusually good, you're probably not misreading it.

Predicted format speed: Medium. Four of the five color pairs play as mid-range decks. Only one — Black/Green — is genuinely aggressive this time around.

Field note: Don't force synergy. Every archetype in this guide rewards playing good cards first and letting the theme (Storied, Landfall, Ferocious, Amass) be a bonus — with a few notable build-around exceptions called out below.

II. Set Mechanics

Storied RW

Once you control three or more of (artifacts, legendaries, sagas), you gain a permanent "enduring story" for the rest of the game — the same "can't be lost once earned" template as Ascend's City's Blessing. Cards with Storied get upgraded once that threshold is hit — mostly a Red/White theme. Common legendaries tend to be cheap creatures that trade off early, but losing the creature doesn't remove the emblem once earned. Tokens count too — a Food or Treasure token is still an artifact for this purpose, so token-makers can help you hit the threshold even with a thin legendary/Saga count. Storied permanents themselves only show up at common/uncommon on legendary creatures — the sagas and other permanents that care about Storied are uncommon/rare-only.

Landfall UG

Triggers whenever a land enters the battlefield under your control — including your normal land drop, so it fires at least once most turns for free. Extra land drops and land-fetch effects let you trigger it more than once a turn. Central to Blue/Green, where it's paired with an Elves sub-theme.

Recruit UW

Draw a card, then discard a card. If the discard is a nonland, create a 1/1 Human Soldier token instead of a +1/+1 counter. A reskinned Connive that favors Human and token payoffs (Blue/White) — but where Connive tends to push a deck toward attacking (counters on your team), Recruit's cheap ground blocker tends to prolong games instead, which is part of why Blue/White leans controlling rather than aggressive. There's nothing wrong with discarding a land if the card in your hand matters more than a 1/1.

Adventure WUBRG

Cards may have an Adventure side, castable only before the creature side is cast. Always cast the Adventure side first — casting the creature first permanently forfeits the Adventure half. Nearly always good in limited since it's two cards in one. Not tied to one color pair — it shows up across all five colors.

Amass Goblins X BR

Put X +1/+1 counters on an Army you control (making a 0/0 Goblin Army token first if needed). Additional Amass triggers grow the same token rather than making new ones — a "grow one threat" mechanic, not a go-wide one. That doesn't mean it's a one-note plan, though: Black/Red actually supports two distinct lines with it — keep piling counters onto one Army and swing with a growing threat, or repeatedly sacrifice it for value via a sac outlet and Amass again to rebuild a fresh one. Central to Black/Red.

Ferocious BG

Checks whether you control a creature with power 4 or greater, granting a bonus if so. Triggers at the beginning of combat or on attack, which pushes the deck to actually get in there rather than sit back. Central to Black/Green — the format's only real aggro pair.

Hone Counters RW

A new counter type that lives on Equipment rather than creatures: each Hone counter adds +1/+0 to whatever the equipment is attached to, on top of the equipment's own bonus. Only appears on two rares in the set, so it won't come up often — but worth recognizing when it does.

III. The Five Pairs

All five supported color pairs, ranked roughly by expected strength.

Black / Red — Amass Goblins BR predicted best

Amass goblin armies, then sacrifice them for value rather than growing one giant creature. A near-identical mechanic in a prior Lord of the Rings set was one of that set's best pairs — expect similar performance here.

Key cards:

  • Bolg of the North — sac a creature to fight, spill excess damage into amass
  • Great Ugly-Looking Goblin — menace lord with a free Adventure-side amass
  • Misty Mountains Raider — amasses 2 on every attack without tapping itself
  • Snowslope Hunter — repeatable sac outlet that impulse-draws
  • Rhovanion Rampager — 3/2 for 3 that saccs a creature on attack for counters equal to its power, then Amasses that much on death — a two-for-one no matter which half of its life cycle you're using
  • Bothersome Noisemaker — 2/2 for 2 that Amasses off every noncreature spell you cast; wants real spell density (~11 creatures is a reasonable floor to still turn this on)
Archetype tip: Trading your army quickly is usually better than growing it into one giant threat — a single big creature dies to one removal spell, but repeatedly cashing in small armies via a sac outlet is far harder to interact with profitably. Tidings of War is a clean 1-mana Amass 1 with flashback for 3R that can enable an aggressive turn-1-into-turn-2 attack.
Black / Green — Ferocious BG the format's only true aggro deck

Cares about Ferocious (a creature with power 4+), with a Wolves/Bears tribal sub-theme layered on top. This is the one genuinely fast deck in an otherwise mid-range format — build low and apply pressure early.

Key cards:

  • The Chief Warg — menace, draws a card every attack once Ferocious
  • Nasty Little Rabbit — a 1-drop that grows itself for free once you're Ferocious
  • Desolation Prowler — +2/+2 for 2 life, no mana — activatable once on your turn and once on the opponent's, so it can threaten combat math twice a round
  • Beorn the Fierce — 6/6 trample finisher that also anthems your other Bears +2/+2 and turns your board into Bears over time
  • Ordinary Bear — a plain vanilla 4/5 for 4; "no abilities" is the point, since it's just a clean, cheap way to have a power-4+ creature in play
  • Wargling / Duskwatch Hunter — cheap early bridges that are fine on their own and scale hard once Ferocious turns on (Wargling grants the whole team trample on attack; Duskwatch Hunter is a same-turn +1/+1 counter that reliably hits 4 power by turn 3)
Archetype tip: Prioritize cards that buff small creatures into Ferocious range, like Troll Negotiations — a rare two-for-one that removes a blocker and enables your payoffs. Aim for roughly 8-10 creatures with power 4+ (or effects that grant it) — and prioritize hitting that count over just adding more raw power, since a curve top-heavy with 6-drop 4/4s can't turn Ferocious on early enough to matter.
Red / White — Dwarves & Storied RW mid-range, not aggro

Normally the format's aggro slot — not this time. Creatures are somewhat understatted on their own and equipment costs extra mana to equip, pushing this into mid-range territory instead.

Don't overbuild around Storied — it's a fine bonus (often just Ward 1) but not worth forcing weak cards into your deck. The real Storied payoffs live at rare/mythic.

Key cards:

  • Thorin Oakenshield — 3/2 trample for 2 that hands out Ward once Storied
  • Bifur, Melodic Rider — doubles your Dwarves' triggered abilities once Storied
  • Dori, Bearer of Friends — 3/2 trample for 3 with a free Treasure — also a common that alone gets you a third of the way to Storied
  • Kíli the Resourceful — draws off every Dwarf/Equipment ETB, frees your first equip a turn
  • Dáin Ironfoot — makes and auto-attaches a free Equipment on ETB, then grants double strike to every equipped attacker — splashable in most decks that touch red
  • Key to the Side-Door — cashes in a redundant legendary draw for two fresh cards, or grants unblockable to force through a suited-up threat; worth up to two copies
Archetype tip: Manage your legendary creature count while drafting and deckbuilding — avoid over-drafting copies of the same legendary so you're not stuck holding two in an opening hand. Iron Hills Blacksmith and Dwarven Shortsword are worth prioritizing specifically as cheap "two pieces of cardboard for one card" equipment that also feeds your Storied count.
Build-around to watch for: An Unexpected Party — a 4-mana (2WW) enchantment that anthems one chosen creature type by +2/+2, with an Adventure sorcery side, At the Door (X2W), that makes X 2/2 red Dwarf tokens. Play At the Door first for a wave of Dwarves, then cast the enchantment naming Dwarf the turn after — those same tokens are suddenly 4/4s. Treat it as a genuine top-ten bomb, not just a build-around footnote.
On splashing: this pair is an exception to the guide's general "light splash is fine" advice (Chapter VIII). Its archetypes read as tight enough "on rails," and its own fixing artifacts smooth your own two colors more than they enable a third, that going past two colors here is rarely worth it.
Blue / White — Humans & Recruit UW control-leaning

Cares about drawing a second card each turn, aided heavily by Recruit. It can play two different ways: as a controlling deck holding up mana and grinding out card advantage, or as a proactive go-wide deck that taps out for Recruit tokens and attacks instead. Which one you end up building probably depends on your specific pool — don't assume it has to be slow.

Key cards:

  • Bard the Bowman — reach blocker that gives lifelink off your 2nd card a turn
  • Lake-town Toymaker — 3/4 that hands out +3/+0 and first strike once you've drawn two
  • Patient Instructor — a common that Recruits on a vigilant 2/2 body
  • Great Gilded Boat — Recruits on every attack, the pair's core engine
  • Esgaroth Garrison — a */5 whose power equals your creature count, plus a Recruit trigger on ETB — gets bigger the wider you go
  • Moment of Glory — a +1/+1 counter for W, or a team-wide +1/+1 with flashback if you're happy discarding it to Recruit first
Archetype tip: Look for cards with flashback or graveyard-recursion (like Eagle's Rescue) that you're happy to discard to Recruit — you get the token now and the spell later, without losing access to a card you wanted.
Blue / Green — Elves & Landfall UG standard ramp shell

The classic landfall pair, with an Elves tribal sub-theme. There aren't a ton of strong landfall payoffs at common, so — as with Storied — don't overload the theme; play good cards and treat landfall as a bonus.

Key cards:

  • Silvan Reveler — loots a land into play, then buys itself back off a later landfall
  • Thranduil, Sindarin Liege — Elf lord that mills for lands off its Adventure side first
  • Mirkwood Pathmaker — a */* that just keeps growing with your land count
  • Wood Elves — tutors a Forest straight onto the battlefield on ETB, triggering landfall immediately — probably the pair's best ramp piece
  • Beorn, Reluctant Host — a real 5/5 trample for 5, with an Adventure side (Till and Tend) that's just an extra land drop — accelerant and payoff on the same card
  • Thranduil's Company — honorable mention: a real 3/4 for 4 (2GU) that hands out an extra land drop with another Elf — but the landfall trigger itself fires off any land you play, Elf or no, so it's a fine include even outside a dedicated Elves shell
Archetype tip: Holding lands to set up big landfall turns is fine — but if you draw a card-draw spell while sitting on lands, you may draw playables you can't afford to cast. Balance how much draw is left in your deck against how many landfall payoffs you're holding for.
Splash base: this pair's land-search effects can fetch off-color duals, which makes it the easiest base in the format to splash a third color from — for example, fixing into Thranduil, the Elvenking (a Sultai/BUG 5/6 that inherits every Elf ability in your graveyard) off a green-black dual.

Board Wipes (none are unconditional — good news)

The scariest is Settle the Wreckage — exiles all attacking creatures. Once known, be cautious about alpha-striking into an opponent holding 4+ mana with no visible reason to hold back. Past that, everything else is a "mini sweeper" that only dents the board rather than clearing it: Desolation of Smaug (a 4-mana rare dealing 3 to every non-Dragon creature, plus a burst of mana for Dragon spells), Gnashing of Teeth (a one-sided -1/-1 to all your opponent's creatures as its second mode), and the Adventure side of Glóin the Mighty (Easy Pickings, -1/-1 to all your opponent's creatures for 2R). Small, so-so against a board of anything with 3+ toughness, but real against a swarm of 1/1 tokens.

Dual Lands~50% of packs — e.g. Elvenking's Halls (Blue/Green)
Hobbit Holesac for a basic now, or discard for 4 to tutor a Halfling later
Giant's BoulderScry 2 on ETB, then a mana rock, then a 7-mana removal spell late
Old Thrushgains 2 life and tops your library with a land — smooths the next draw
Thrór's Mapfinds a land to hand immediately, then loots every turn after — a fine 22nd/23rd card, not a high pick
Troop of Poniesa real 2/1 first, a double land-fetch later — never dead, but also a low-priority late pick

Dual lands enter tapped for either color of their pair, and can later be sacrificed for 4 mana to add counters to a relevant creature type — good in two-color decks and light splashes alike. That counter-payoff mode matters more the faster your deck is: turning a combat-relevant creature from, say, 4/4 into 6/6 (sometimes with trample) on the spot is scarier in Red/White or Black/Green than in the format's slower pairs.

Sequencing tip: against an aggressive opponent, playing a 2-mana fixing artifact like Thrór's Map on curve turn 2 can cost you real tempo — consider holding it and deploying it turn 4-5 instead, once the board state calls for it less urgently.

VIII. Field Notes for Prerelease

  1. Run at least six removal spells. With 6 packs plus a guaranteed foil rare per player, you'll face a minimum of 7 rares across the table — and individual Play Boosters can contain 2-3 rares each, so the realistic number you'll see is often higher. (Wizards' own baseline guidance is lighter — 4-5 removal spells against 13-15 creatures in a 23-spell/17-land 40-card deck — but veteran drafters here consistently recommend leaning heavier on removal than that official floor.)
  2. Combat tricks and bounce spells count as removal if you're light on true removal — a bounce-to-library effect costs the opponent a redraw.
  3. Don't be afraid to splash a single pip of a removal spell in a third color — just avoid double-pip splashes. That said, this set rewards splashing less than most: the archetypes are tight enough, and most fixing smooths your own two colors rather than truly enabling a third, that strict two-color is simply better here more often than usual.
  4. Stick to two colors with at most a light splash, rather than three colors outright, to reduce mulligans and awkward hands.
  5. You don't need many two-drops. Four or five is plenty in this mid-range format — include 1-2 card-draw effects instead, since there aren't many late-game mana sinks to fall back on.
  6. Don't force synergy. Play good cards first and let Storied, Landfall, Ferocious, and Amass be bonuses — except for the specific build-around bombs called out above.
  7. Watch for "do-nothing" trap turns. Several rares ask you to skip a turn to set up value — risky in this format's tempo, and worth noticing if an opponent does it too.
  8. Expect the aggro/mid-range roles to be swapped: Red/White plays mid-range this time, and Black/Green is the format's real aggro deck.
  9. The "threat test": when sorting a fresh pool, ask how you'd feel if each rare/mythic were played against you. If the answer is "scared," you've found a real threat — a quick, honest way to spot which color(s) your pool actually wants to be in.
  10. Your promo card counts. The traditional foil promo in your prerelease kit is part of your legal sealed pool — play it if it's strong, same as any other card.
Prerelease etiquette: turn your cards to face your opponent so they can read them, don't sweat early-round takebacks, call a judge if a ruling confuses the table, and don't worry if your card recognition isn't perfect on release weekend — nobody's is. It's meant to be a casual, friendly event, not a competitive one.

IX. A Second Read

A second pass over the same card pool covered in Chapters III–VIII. Where it lines up with the rest of the guide, that's folded in as a stronger signal on those calls. Where it disagrees, both readings are laid out below so you can judge for yourself rather than take either one on faith.

Where the Case Is Genuinely Split

Most cards check out cleanly one way or the other. These five don't — the case for and against each is close enough that reasonable drafters land in different places:

Belladonna Took — Chapter VII's Trap Rares list is skeptical: she only really pays off on a token's 2nd/3rd trigger that turn, so most games she's just a 2/2 for 2. There's a more favorable read, though: in a White deck that's already generating tokens as its game plan (which is most of them), those extra triggers show up more often than the trap label suggests, and she's worth a much longer look than a flat "avoid." There's also a harsher read than Chapter VII's own: without a specifically synergistic pool, she may never actually draw you a card at all. Treat her as a genuine toss-up rather than a settled call — her value swings entirely on how many token-makers are actually in your deck.
The Eagles Are Coming! — Chapter VII calls this a build-around with too narrow a best case to bank on, and there's a real case for that: "only useful to protect your own stuff" has historically been a weak pattern for flicker-style cards. But there's also a strong case that this is one of the set's biggest bombs outright, not just a fringe playable: kicked for three-plus creatures, it turns your whole board into 4/4 flying tokens next upkeep, a real one-card blowout either offensively (bounce your team, swing back with an air force) or defensively (block first, then rebuild bigger). Worth a real bump if your deck already runs a lot of creatures — but genuinely contested, not a settled call either way.
Gigantic Big Bear — an unanswerable finisher on paper: hexproof, haste, can't be countered, hitting for half an opponent's life total out of nowhere. That's all true, but Chapter VII's caveat matters more than it first appears — with no trample, a single 1/1 can chump-block it forever, and some call that outright fatal: your opponent can just keep making one-one tokens and block this for eternity. It's a house when unblocked and a non-event when it isn't.
The Misty Mountains Cold — Chapter VII files this in Tier 1 as a straightforward mana rebate for a 6/6 flier. A more cautious read treats it as Tier 2 at best: without a way to make Treasures independent of the Saga itself, you need favorable draw order to actually chain into a second or third Dragon, and the first one alone takes four turns to arrive.
Thorin, Mountain-king — Chapter VII keeps him in Tier 2, wary of needing Equipment already in play. There's a much higher read on him, though: with Equipment density as high as it is in Red decks generally, "one equipment anywhere on board" is a low bar to clear for a free kill-on-ETB plus a scary trampler — arguably a top-10 bomb outright.
Thranduil's Company — Chapter VII calls this "awkward off-curve" (Tier 2). There's a strong case it deserves better: it's worth splashing as a plain 5-drop 3/4 even with zero Elf synergy in the deck — the "awkward" read may be undervaluing its floor.

Is Red/White Actually Mid-range?

Chapters I and III both call Red/White the exception to this set's usual aggro slot — a mid-range deck built around Storied rather than a straightforward beatdown. Not everyone reads it that way: one source argues Storied basically happens on its own as a byproduct of curving out normally, and that a Red/White pool should be played just as aggressively as the format's other fast decks, treating the enduring-story payoff as a bonus rather than a reason to slow down. Both reads agree on the actual deckbuilding advice (curve out, play good cards, don't force Storied) — they only disagree on how hard to lean into attacking once you've done that. If your pool clearly wants to race, don't let "this set's Red/White is slower" talk you out of it.

Sleeper Picks — Artifacts

Artifacts are quietly pushed a notch above their usual power level in this set, likely to compensate for having only five supported color pairs. Dwarven Mattock auto-equips itself to a Dwarf you control for free, which is a real prize in Red/White given how much that pair already leans on equipment and Storied. Thrór's Map and Giant's Boulder (both already covered in Chapter VI) round out the pattern: good to keep a sketchy hand early, good to filter extra lands late, splashable in any color. The pattern holds at common, too, in a color-neutral run of cheap equipment: Ragged Short Spear (loots on ETB), Crude Bent Blade (an ETB edict), Dwarven Shortsword (makes and attaches its own Dwarf), and Goblin Plate Mail (Amasses and attaches itself) all read like they'd be uncommons in a normal set.

A related caveat on The Arkenstone: its own built-in card draw is often good enough that hard-casting it (or just playing a curve-filling 2-drop instead) beats detouring through its Adventure tutor — treat Seek the Heart as a bonus mode, not the reason to play the card.

Two-Color Signposts (no strong rare? first-pick these)

When pack 1 pick 1 comes down to "no bomb, what's the safest signal to send," these three over-deliver on rate alone: Silvan Reveler (Blue/Green — discard a land and net a card, then do it again if it dies and comes back), Patient Instructor (Blue/White, a common that still over-delivers at three mana), and Fearsome Goblin Pair (Black/Red hybrid mana — five power worth of stats for three mana, plus sacrifice fodder).

Single-Color Power Ranking

A different axis from Chapter III's archetype-pair ranking — this ranks the five colors in isolation, independent of which pair they end up drafted in.

  1. Red — the deepest uncommon slot in the set plus the cheapest removal; edges out Black by a hair.
  2. Black — just as deep at common and uncommon, but its removal wants double black and doesn't splash as easily.
  3. Blue — very deep at common, noticeably thinner at uncommon; a solid floor without a high ceiling.
  4. Green — strong uncommons, but only one or two commons worth playing, and its fixing is unusually narrow this time (an Elf-only mana dork, a fetch that only finds Forests) — normally Green's job is helping you splash, and this set's version doesn't.
  5. White — the clear worst color this time, a reversal from its usual best-in-format spot in recent Standard-adjacent limited formats.
Reconciling with Chapter III: this isn't a contradiction of "Black/Green is the only real aggro deck" — a color can be individually powerful while its best archetype still plays as mid-range. Red and Black are both strong in isolation, but the pair they share leans sacrifice-value, not top-end speed.
Because Red, Black, and Blue are the three strongest colors in isolation, don't feel locked into only the five supported pairs above if your pool points elsewhere. Red/Blue and Blue/Black aren't official archetypes with their own payoffs, but both are "quite deep" on card quality alone, and a splash into the third of those three colors (Blue/Black/Red — the classic three-color "Grixis" shard) is worth keeping in mind if you end up light on a coherent two-color plan but heavy on good cards in those three.

Easiest to Pilot (a beginner-friendly axis)

A third ranking, separate from both "best archetype" and "best color" above — this one's about how forgiving a pair is to actually play, useful if you're bringing a newer player to the table.

  1. Black/Green (Ferocious) — easiest. Fewest moving parts: get a big creature down, turn Ferocious on, attack.
  2. Blue/White (Recruit) — straightforward card-advantage engine that mostly plays itself.
  3. Red/White (Storied) — "you almost can't lose it — it happens by accident" once you're holding a few Dwarves and artifacts.
  4. Black/Red (Amass) — reads confusing on the stack the first time, but clicks permanently after one game.
  5. Blue/Green (Landfall) — hardest. The most sequencing-dependent deck in the set: managing land drops, holding cards, and timing landfall triggers takes real practice.

Table Math and Removal Density

Two structural observations worth keeping in mind while building: first, on an 8-person pod you'll typically see roughly half of each printed rare, so 3-4 bombs concentrated in one color (not unusual — White in particular tends to cluster bombs in this set) can swing a whole draft on their own. Second, this format is unusually light on exile-based removal and true board wipes outside White's reprinted Settle the Wreckage — which makes death-trigger and graveyard-value cards (Tom, Bert, and William, Rhovanion Rampager) safer includes than they'd be in a format with more exile effects. Between the removal gap and the density of anthem/token effects at uncommon and rare, going wide is rewarded more than usual this time.

On Settle the Wreckage specifically: it's easy to overrate from nostalgia for the original printing. In practice it often only catches one or two attackers (the rest of your board just gets exiled for the caster's benefit while your opponent fetches replacement lands), and a good opponent plays around it once it's known. Chapter V's caution about not alpha-striking into open White mana still holds — just don't expect it to be a bigger blowout than it usually is.
